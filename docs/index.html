Model validation vs NBER recessions

Forecast skill measured by replaying the model month-by-month and scoring its alerts against NBER-dated recessions. These are earned metrics, not asserted — the alert thresholds are doctrinal, so this is how they actually perform.

Hit rate = share of recession months flagged ≥ the alert level. False-positive rate = share of expansion months wrongly flagged. Lead = months of continuous warning before each recession began. Brier = mean squared error of composite/100 as a probability (lower is better; 0.25 = coin flip).

Out-of-sample test

The honest test: alert cutoffs are optimized on the older half of history (training: pre-2005 recessions), frozen, then scored on the held-out recent recessions the cutoffs never saw (test: 2007–09 GFC, 2020). A small train→test skill drop means the signal generalizes rather than overfits. ROC AUC is threshold-independent (0.5 = no skill, 1.0 = perfect).

Robustness: walk-forward + bootstrap CI

A single split gives one number with no uncertainty. Walk-forward retrains at rolling origins so every recession is held out in some fold; the moving-block bootstrap puts a 95% confidence interval on ROC AUC, honestly reflecting the small recession sample. Wide intervals = treat the skill estimate with caution.

Composite score · 30-year backtest

Each point = end-of-month replay using only data available at that cutoff (no look-ahead). Shaded bands = NBER-dated US recessions.

Composite score NBER recession 30 = YELLOW threshold 60 = RED threshold

How to read this: the model is calibrated for relative signal monitoring, not point-prediction. A useful model should spend most pre-recession periods in YELLOW or RED before NBER calls a recession, and most expansion periods in GREEN. Crossings into RED during expansions are false positives; ideally there are few.

Limitations: the z-score window is 60 months (5 years), so very early backtest years have noisier scores. Layer weights are doctrinal, not optimized against NBER chronology, so this is a sanity check rather than a hold-out evaluation.

Key Economist Frameworks

Sahm Rule

Recession begins when the 3-month moving average of national unemployment rate rises by 0.5 percentage points or more relative to its low during the previous 12 months. Currently tracked as SAHMREALTIME in FRED.

Estrella-Mishkin Probit

Academic model (1996) using the 10Y-3M Treasury yield spread to estimate 12-month recession probability. Formula: Φ(−0.307 − 0.667 × spread). This dashboard includes it in the ensemble score.

El-Erian's "New Normal"

Post-GFC thesis that growth, rates, and inflation would remain structurally suppressed. The 2022–2024 inflation shock and rate cycle tested this framework; his current focus is on AI productivity offsets vs. deglobalization drag.

Roubini's Systemic Risk

Tracks "megathreats" — geopolitical fragmentation, debt overhangs, demographic decline. His signal set overlaps with this dashboard's global layer: OECD CLI, EU yield curve, and credit spread monitors.

Greeks Reference

Delta (Δ)

Rate of change of the option price with respect to the underlying price. A call delta of 0.60 means the option gains $0.60 for every $1 rise in the stock. Delta ∈ [0,1] for calls, [−1,0] for puts. Also approximates the probability of expiring in-the-money.

Gamma (Γ)

Rate of change of Delta. High gamma means delta changes rapidly as the stock moves — ATM options near expiry have the highest gamma. Gamma risk is highest for short-dated positions. Long options have positive gamma (you benefit from large moves).

Theta (Θ)

Time decay — how much the option loses per day as expiration approaches. Negative for long options (you lose value each day). ATM options decay fastest near expiry. Theta = Vega's opponent: selling options earns theta but risks vega loss if IV spikes.

Vega (ν)

Sensitivity to implied volatility. A vega of 0.20 means the option gains $0.20 for every 1% rise in IV. Long options benefit from rising IV (VIX spikes). During recessions, IV surges — long put positions profit from both delta and vega simultaneously.

Rho (ρ)

Sensitivity to interest rates. A 1% rise in rates increases a call option by rho and decreases a put by rho. Rho matters most for long-dated options (LEAPS). Fed rate cuts (RED composite) reduce call rho; rate hikes do the opposite.

Options & Macro Context

When the composite is RED, buying puts on broad market ETFs (SPY, QQQ) can hedge portfolio risk. Rising VIX inflates premiums — buying options in GREEN conditions (low VIX, cheap premiums) to hedge against future RED scenarios is the classic "disaster insurance" trade.

Scoring approach

Threshold-based indicators

For indicators with a defined threshold (e.g. yield curve at 0, Sahm Rule at 0.5), risk is computed as the signed distance from the threshold, passed through a logistic function: 1 / (1 + e^(−4x)). Crossing the threshold gives a score of 50.

Z-score indicators

For indicators without a fixed threshold (e.g. unemployment, GDP), a z-score is computed over a rolling 36-month window. The z-score is then passed through the same logistic function. Mean = 50, ±1σ ≈ 73 and 27.

Alert states

Composite score 0–29: GREEN (low risk). 30–59: YELLOW (elevated watch). 60–100: RED (high risk). Thresholds reflect judgment, not backtested calibration.

History backfill

On each data refresh, the full 24-month history is recomputed by replaying monthly snapshots against the FRED series, using only data available at each cutoff date (real-time z-score windows). No look-ahead bias.

Limitations: Layer weights are doctrinal, not empirically optimized against NBER chronology — the composite score itself is not fit to the data. Alert thresholds are calibrated by intuition, but the Backtest tab now reports an out-of-sample test: cutoffs are optimized on pre-2005 recessions, frozen, and scored on the held-out 2007–09 and 2020 recessions, alongside a threshold-independent ROC AUC. Series have different release lags (daily ≈ 1 day stale, monthly ≈ 1–2 months, quarterly ≈ 3–4 months) and the composite blends these without stale-adjusting.
